IT21296482 · S A H Samarathunga

Market Modeling and Explainable AI Insight Generation

Research problem

Retail investors often receive raw prices, volumes, and technical indicators without an accessible explanation of the movement, its unusualness, or its uncertainty.

Contribution in plain English

I learn a stock's usual behaviour from its historical prices and trading volume, estimate a possible future price path, and compare expected prices with actual observations. I show how large and unusual a difference is, which factors influenced the anomaly score, and whether those explanations stay consistent over time.

Methodology

  1. Validate and order historical observations
  2. Engineer trend, volatility, and liquidity features
  3. Train on earlier observations; calibrate on validation data
  4. Evaluate later observations against an unchanged-price baseline
  5. Return forecasts, anomalies, explanations, and limitations
Key finding

Explainability must remain alongside the forecast: a model association can describe how the model used a variable, but cannot prove that the variable caused a market movement.

Individual research evidence / IT21296482

From an expected price to an explainable anomaly

This is a static academic record of the implemented calculations, not a live stock-analysis service. Expand each topic to inspect the method and its limitations.

01 / Data, training, and forecast logic

Inputs: dated open, high, low, close, and trading-volume observations for the selected CSE stock. The preserved experiments cover JKH and BIL. The source metadata records a TradingView CSELK fallback; the dataset must not be described as exclusively collected from Ideabeam.

Preparation: order dates, check duplicates, missing values, impossible prices and negative volume, then build returns, moving averages, volatility, momentum, and volume-ratio features. The default sequence uses the previous 20 observations to estimate the next closing price. Scaling is fitted on training rows only. The chronological split is approximately 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% testing.

Forecasting: an LSTM sequence model learns expected closing-price behaviour. A forecast ensemble combines the LSTM scenario, an unchanged-price path, and a moving-average drift path. Weights are based on validation error, not test results. The live runtime generates a fresh 120-trading-session path, with checkpoints at 4, 20, 60, and 120 sessions (approximately 4 days, 1, 3, and 6 months).

wi = [1 / max(MAEi,validation, ε)] / Σj[1 / max(MAEj,validation, ε)]
Forecastt = Σi wi × Forecasti,t

The favourable and adverse paths are uncertainty ranges, not guaranteed maximum/minimum prices. A six-month output is available, but it is not evidence of a completed six-month prospective validation.

On Analyze in the application: the backend refreshes available market observations, loads the selected stock's historical MongoDB rows, exports a private temporary CSV, reruns the market pipeline, and returns a run ID and data cutoff. Temporary files are deleted. Locked May forecasts are used for research validation only, never substituted for a fresh live result.

02 / Deviation and liquidity-aware anomaly score

Let A be the actual closing price (LKR), P the expected closing price (LKR), and V the number of shares traded on the observation date.

Signed deviation D = A − P
Absolute deviation d = |A − P|
Signed deviation (%) = 100 × (A − P) / P
Scaled volume v = V / 1,000,000
Liquidity-aware anomaly score S = d / (v + ε), where ε = 0.000001

A negative signed deviation means the stock closed below the model estimate; a positive value means it closed above it. The percentage is relative to the expected price, and is undefined if P is zero. The score is not a percentage or a probability.

Worked example (illustrative, not a live JKH quote)

Expected price = Rs. 24.00; actual price = Rs. 19.80; volume = 2,000,000 shares.

D = 19.80 − 24.00 = −4.20 LKR
d = |−4.20| = 4.20 LKR
Deviation (%) = (−4.20 / 24.00) × 100 = −17.50%
v = 2,000,000 / 1,000,000 = 2
S = 4.20 / (2 + 0.000001) ≈ 2.099999

If the calibrated score threshold for this example were 0.446011, S would exceed it and trigger the liquidity flag. That number is an example threshold, not a universal constant or the current threshold for every stock.

Why volume matters: the same price gap produces a larger score when fewer shares trade. A large score may therefore indicate thin trading, not strong market-wide confirmation. The small ε avoids division by zero; zero-volume and thin-trading flags must still qualify interpretation.

Relative volume = V / rolling 20-observation median volume
Low volume: relative volume < 0.50
Thin trading: V = 0 or relative volume < 0.25
Market-confirmed diagnostic C = d × ln(1 + v)

C increases with participation and is a separate supporting diagnostic. It is not a replacement for S and is not directly used in the final OR rule below.

03 / How the threshold and final anomaly flag are selected

The anomaly threshold is calibrated, not manually chosen and not learned as a forecast target. Candidate liquidity cutoffs come from the 60th to 99.5th percentiles of training/validation scores. Candidate prediction-band cutoffs are also searched, with a minimum severity threshold of 1.05.

Each candidate pair is checked on the chronological validation split against proxy anomaly labels: predefined event windows, or extreme returns accompanied by high volatility or a volume spike. The selection first maximizes F0.5, which weights precision more heavily than recall; ties consider precision, excessive flag rates, rate consistency, accuracy, and recall. Test observations are evaluated after selection.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP); Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
F0.5 = 1.25 × Precision × Recall / (0.25 × Precision + Recall)

Prediction bands: validation residuals determine asymmetric downside/upside widths. The base width is the larger of the 95th percentile of absolute validation residuals, their standard deviation, and 0.000001. Each side width is at least the base width.

Downside gap = max(P − A, 0); Upside gap = max(A − P, 0)
Band severity B = max(downside gap / downside width, upside gap / upside width)
Liquidity flag = (S ≥ Tliquidity)
Band flag = (B ≥ Tband)
Final anomaly = Liquidity flag OR Band flag

A price outside the basic band does not automatically satisfy the calibrated band-severity cutoff. A separate structural discount signal is not included in this OR rule.

Recorded example: the packaged JKH dashboard artifact reports Tliquidity = 0.961452. Its calibration metadata records a selected liquidity percentile of 95.4586. These belong to that saved run; rerunning with different observations can change the selected thresholds.

Evaluation limitation: proxy labels are not independently verified ground truth. Their tail quantiles are currently constructed on the prepared full series, so claims of fully leakage-free anomaly-label validation would be too strong. Split-local label construction and independently reviewed events remain necessary follow-up checks.

04 / Structural gap, market pressure, and interpretation

The component keeps an unusual observation separate from a price being below its historical-pattern reference.

Structural reference R = 0.35 × SMA20 + 0.25 × SMA50 + 0.25 × EMA12 + 0.15 × P
Structural discount = max(R − A, 0)
Structural discount fraction = structural discount / R

SMA means simple moving average; EMA means exponential moving average. Missing averages fall back to P. The structural-gap cutoff is the 90th percentile of positive training/validation discount fractions. This is a pattern-based reference, not fair value and not proof that a share should recover.

A separate pressure layer combines standardized unusualness in 1-day and 5-day returns, 20-observation volatility, the volume ratio, and S. Optional external proxies are included only when available.

zj = |(xj − μj) / σj|
Pressure score = Σj zj
Pressure threshold = 90th percentile of training/validation pressure scores

Means and population standard deviations come from training/validation rows; a nonpositive or invalid standard deviation uses 1. Missing pressure inputs contribute zero and must be disclosed. This pressure score is different from the liquidity score and cannot establish that war, news, or another external event caused a deviation.

05 / Explanations, stability, and the research novelty

A Random Forest regression surrogate approximates the liquidity-aware anomaly score. Tree SHAP identifies which inputs influenced that surrogate output. It does not directly explain the internal LSTM forecast. If SHAP is unavailable, the implementation labels its feature-importance fallback; it must not be presented as Tree SHAP.

Surrogate output f(x) = baseline + Σj φj
Displayed contribution sharej = |φj| / Σk|φk|

The normalized shares show influence magnitude, not a factor's causal effect or its direction. Signed raw contributions retain the direction relative to the surrogate baseline.

Explanation Stability Index (ESI): rank factors by average absolute attribution within each 60-observation window, advancing by 20 observations. Compare the top three factor names in consecutive windows.

ESIt = |Top3t ∩ Top3t−1| / 3
Example: {volume, volatility, return} and {volume, volatility, momentum}
ESI = 2 / 3 ≈ 0.667 (moderate stability)

ESI ≥ 0.80 is high, 0.50 to below 0.80 moderate, and below 0.50 low. No pair of windows means stability cannot be measured. ESI checks top-factor overlap, not forecast accuracy, attribution-sign stability, or user understanding. A stable explanation can still be dominated by volume.

Claimed contribution

The research contribution is the combined CSE-focused workflow: expected-price modelling, volume-adjusted deviation scoring, a separately calibrated band flag, separate structural-gap interpretation, surrogate explanations, and temporal explanation checks. LSTM, Random Forest, and SHAP themselves are established methods, not newly invented algorithms.

What is not yet established: worldwide novelty, superior forecasting accuracy, causal explanations, and validated feature-interaction effects. Formal interaction analysis should remain a research objective, not a completed result. The evidence below supports reproducibility and an integrated method, not a claim that every prediction is correct.

RMSE = √mean((A − P)2)
MAPE (%) = mean(|A − P| / |A|) × 100

MAPE divides by actual price, unlike the signed deviation percentage above. These are different quantities.

IT22065308 · Harithra K

Sentiment and Event-Driven Financial Insight Modelling

Research Problem

Financial news contains valuable information about market sentiment, but manually interpreting large volumes of news is time-consuming. General sentiment models may also struggle to understand financial terminology and market-related events.

Research Gap

General text classification may not fully capture the context of financial news. Combining machine learning with financial rule-based analysis offers an approach to improve sentiment interpretation for the Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE).

Research Aim

To develop and evaluate a hybrid sentiment analysis model that classifies financial news and supports sentiment-based market insights for the CSE.

Research Objectives

  • Classify financial news into positive, negative, and neutral sentiment.
  • Develop a Naive Bayes baseline classifier.
  • Integrate machine learning predictions with financial rule-based analysis.
  • Compare model performance using classification metrics.
  • Integrate sentiment results into the stock market insight platform.

Research Questions

  • How effectively can financial news be classified into sentiment categories?
  • Can financial rules improve machine learning-based sentiment classification?
  • How can sentiment analysis support CSE-related market insights?

Proposed Methodology

Individual contribution

This component combines a financial-news classifier with domain-specific rules, then compares the hybrid output with the classifier alone. Its contribution is a CSE-focused sentiment workflow and a documented comparison, not a claim to have invented sentiment analysis.

The proposed workflow combines natural language processing, machine learning, and financial rule-based logic to produce a final sentiment classification.

01. News Preprocessing

Financial news headlines are collected and prepared for analysis through text preprocessing.

02. Machine Learning Classification

A Naive Bayes classifier, implemented using the natural JavaScript library, predicts the sentiment of each headline.

03. Financial Rule-Based Analysis

Financial terms and domain-specific rules are analysed to identify sentiment signals that may require additional financial context.

04. Hybrid Decision

The machine learning prediction is combined with rule-based evidence. Strong financial rule signals can influence the final sentiment decision.

05. Evaluation and Integration

The Naive Bayes and hybrid models are evaluated using a labelled dataset of 60 financial headlines. The resulting sentiment insights are integrated into the web application.

Hybrid Sentiment Model

The model combines machine learning predictions with financial rule-based evidence to determine the final sentiment classification.

Model Evaluation Metrics

The classification performance of both models is measured using the following evaluation metrics.

01. Accuracy

02. Precision

03. Recall

04. F1-Score

05. Macro F1-Score

Macro F1-Score = F1Positive + F1Negative + F1Neutral 3

Experimental Evaluation

Both models were evaluated on the same dataset of 60 labelled financial headlines, consisting of 20 positive, 20 negative, and 20 neutral examples.

Evaluation Metric Naive Bayes Hybrid Model

Key Finding

The hybrid model improved accuracy by 3.33 percentage points and achieved a modest increase in macro F1-score. However, neutral sentiment recall decreased, indicating that further refinement is needed to improve performance across all sentiment categories.

Technology Stack

Sentiment Analysis: Natural (Naive Bayes), financial rule-based logic
Frontend Integration: React, Vite, Axios

Research Outcome

The component demonstrates a hybrid approach to financial news sentiment classification, combining machine learning and financial rule-based analysis to generate sentiment insights for CSE-related market analysis. These insights support interpretation of financial news rather than predicting stock prices or providing investment advice.

IT22547088 · T G R N S Wijesooriya

Explainable AI-Based Financial Market Risk Impact Engine

Research Problem

Retail investors may see changes in stock prices and trading volume, but they may not understand the current risk condition. A risk label alone does not explain why the model produced that result.

Research Gap

Prices, stock indicators, and global market indicators are often viewed separately. A model's overall feature importance also does not explain one selected result. This component brings these inputs and a local explanation together in one CSE-focused workflow.

Research Aim

To develop and evaluate an explainable system that classifies the current risk condition of selected CSE stocks as LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH, and explains the main factors behind each result.

Individual Contribution

I combine stock data and global indicators, use Random Forest to classify risk, use SHAP to explain the result, and use Gemini to write a simple explanation. These outputs support our overall CSE insight system.

Research Objectives

  1. Collect and prepare historical and current stock data.
  2. Calculate stock features such as moving averages and volatility.
  3. Add Gold, Oil, and VIX as global market indicators.
  4. Classify current risk using a Random Forest model.
  5. Explain individual results using SHAP.
  6. Generate simple text from the risk result and SHAP factors.
  7. Connect the workflow to the Flask API and React/Vite dashboard.
  8. Evaluate classification, explanations, integration, and limitations.

Research Questions

  1. How can stock and global indicators be combined for risk analysis?
  2. How well does the model separate the prototype risk classes?
  3. Which features influence each selected prediction?
  4. How can the result be explained clearly to a non-expert user?

Research Methodology

The workflow prepares market features, classifies the current risk state, explains the model result, and presents it in the dashboard.

Individual contribution

The contribution combines a risk class with gold, oil and VIX context and a local explanation of the selected result. It complements Component 1's expected-price deviations: a risk class and a price anomaly answer different questions. The classifiers and explanation methods themselves are established techniques. This section records the member's standalone prototype; the architecture page describes the wider integrated application.

01. Data Preparation

Order historical stock records by symbol and date. The archived dataset contains 33,080 rows from 2020 to 2023. The dashboard supports HHL, JKH, and LLUB.

02. Feature Engineering

Prepare Close, Volume, MA10, MA50, Volatility, Gold, Oil, VIX, and Stock_encoded. Training volatility is the ten-period rolling standard deviation of returns.

03. Risk Classification

A saved Random Forest model with 200 trees returns LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH. Prototype training labels are created from volatility bands.

04. Explainability

SHAP TreeExplainer identifies features that influenced the predicted class. These contributions explain the model's result; they do not prove that a feature caused a market movement.

05. Plain-Language Explanation

Gemini 2.5 Flash writes a simple explanation using the structured risk result and main SHAP factors. Gemini does not choose or change the risk class.

06. Evaluation and Integration

Inspect the archived classification metrics and model explanations. Connect the result to the dashboard through the Flask API. Report limitations alongside the findings.

Recorded result

The archived confusion matrix shows 100% classification accuracy on one reported split of 6,291 observations. Volatility accounts for approximately 88.18% of the saved model's impurity-based feature importance. Because the labels were constructed from volatility bands and Volatility is also an input, this result demonstrates reproduction of the prototype rule; it does not establish perfect future-risk prediction.

0.01 ≤ Volatility < 0.03 → MEDIUM

Interpretation: using Volatility both to construct target labels and as a model input creates a circular relationship. High classification scores therefore do not validate forecasts of future losses. The thesis also reports no formal human-subject comprehension study, and the prototype dashboard confidence percentage is a placeholder rather than a validated evaluation metric.

The final thesis also identifies a preprocessing mismatch: training uses rolling return volatility, while the live endpoint uses the standard deviation of recent prices. The missing final training notebook limits full reproduction of the evaluation split. Historical replay checks confirm that the persisted model accepts the intended inputs for HHL, JKH, and LLUB. Future work includes independently defined future-risk targets, chronological out-of-sample evaluation, suitable classifier baselines, verified multiclass SHAP handling, and controlled user-comprehension testing.

05 / Explain this component
My component in one minute

My name is T G R N S Wijesooriya. My student ID is IT22547088. My component is the Explainable AI-Based Financial Market Risk Impact Engine. It helps users understand the current risk condition of a selected stock. It uses stock data and global indicators such as Gold, Oil, and VIX. A Random Forest model gives a LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH risk label. SHAP shows which features influenced that result. Gemini turns the result into a simple English explanation. My component adds risk information to our overall CSE insight system.

How does it work?

First, the user selects a supported stock. The backend prepares nine inputs. The saved Random Forest model gives a risk class. Next, SHAP finds the main factors behind that class. Finally, the dashboard shows the risk result and an explanation. Gemini writes the explanation; it does not choose the risk class.

Why did you use Random Forest?

Random Forest combines many decision trees. Our saved model has 200 trees. It can work with different market features, and it can be explained using Tree SHAP.

What do the main terms mean?

Volatility means how much returns change over time. MA10 and MA50 are average prices over 10 and 50 observations. VIX is a global market uncertainty indicator. SHAP shows how each feature contributes to a model result.

What is your contribution?

I combine a risk class, local feature explanations, and global market context in one workflow. The user can see both the result and the factors behind it. I use existing methods; I did not invent Random Forest or SHAP.

What are the limitations and future improvements?

The current labels depend on volatility rules. Training used volatility of returns, but the live endpoint uses variation in recent prices. These calculations should be made consistent. The final training notebook was not available for full reproduction. We have not completed a formal user-understanding study. In future, I would use independently defined future-risk labels, test on later unseen dates, compare other models, and test the explanations with users.

How does it help the full project?

Other components provide market modelling, financial-report information, and news sentiment. My component provides risk information and its explanation. Together, these outputs give the user more context about a stock.
